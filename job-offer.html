The hard part

Getting a Canadian job offer from overseas

Most provincial streams need one, and this is where nearly everyone stalls. The honest version: cold-applying to job boards from abroad has a very low success rate. But the reason it fails is specific, and once you understand it you can aim at the places where it does not apply.

Why your applications are being ignored

It is rarely about you. Put yourself on the other side of the desk.

A Canadian employer who wants to hire someone overseas normally has to get a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) — a government ruling that no Canadian could do the job. That means advertising the role for weeks, documenting every Canadian applicant they rejected and why, paying a fee of around $1,000 CAD, and waiting months for a decision that might be refused. Then you still need a work permit, which takes months more.

So when a manager needs someone in six weeks and your application arrives from another continent, they are not weighing your skills against a local candidate’s. They are weighing you plus nine months of paperwork and a four-figure bill against a local candidate who can start on Monday.

Which reframes the whole problem

Your task is not to be the best applicant. It is to find the situations where that friction is small or absent — and then to be a good applicant there. Everything below is a way of doing that.

Route one: programs where the employer needs no LMIA

This is the highest-leverage thing on this page, and most people never learn it exists.

The Atlantic Immigration Program

Employers in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, PEI and Newfoundland can become designated employers. Once designated, they can hire you from overseas with no LMIA at all. No advertising requirement, no $1,000 fee, no months of waiting for ESDC.

Each province publishes its list of designated employers. That list is a list of companies that have already decided they want to hire internationally and have done the paperwork to make it easy. Applying to them is a completely different proposition from applying cold.

  • Find each province’s designated employer list on its immigration website.
  • Apply to them directly, and say in your first line that you are applying under the Atlantic Immigration Program — many smaller employers have designation but do not think to use it.
  • Atlantic Canada has an ageing population and genuine labour shortages. The competition is a fraction of Toronto’s.

Rural and Francophone Community Immigration Pilots

Fourteen rural communities and six francophone communities select their own candidates. Each publishes designated employers and the occupations it needs. You need a job offer from a designated employer inside the community boundary, plus a recommendation from the community itself.

These communities are small, they are trying to attract people, and the offices that run them are staffed by people who answer emails. That is unusual in immigration and worth using. Some hold information sessions for overseas candidates.

Other LMIA-exempt paths worth checking

  • Francophone Mobility — French-speaking workers heading anywhere outside Quebec can get an LMIA-exempt work permit. For a francophone this removes the single biggest obstacle on this page.
  • Intra-company transfer — if your current employer has any Canadian presence, this is often the easiest route in existence. Ask.
  • Free trade agreements — CUSMA (for Mexican and US citizens), CETA, CPTPP and others create LMIA-exempt categories for certain professionals.
  • Global Talent Stream — for high-skilled technology roles, with a two-week processing service standard. It still involves an LMIA, but a fast-tracked one, and tech employers know it well. If you are in software or data, name it in your outreach.

Route two: employers who recruit internationally on purpose

Some organisations run permanent overseas recruitment because they cannot fill roles domestically. They have staff whose job is to hire you.

  • Provincial health authorities. Nova Scotia Health, BC’s health authorities, Saskatchewan Health Authority and others recruit nurses, physicians and allied health staff abroad, often with relocation support and help through licensing. Go to their careers pages directly rather than through job boards.
  • Long-term care and home care operators, which face severe shortages nationwide.
  • Trucking companies, particularly in the Prairie provinces.
  • Meat processing and food production in rural Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba — long-standing LMIA users who know the process well.
  • Agriculture, seasonal and permanent.
  • Construction, where several provinces have created dedicated streams because the shortage is acute.

Also watch for provincial recruitment missions. Saskatchewan, Manitoba, New Brunswick and others send delegations to job fairs overseas, often in the Philippines, India, the UK, Ireland, France and francophone Africa. Being recruited at one of these can count as a connection to the province in its own right. Provincial immigration websites announce them in advance — check the news page of any province you are targeting.

Making your application work

The Canadian résumé is not your CV

  • No photo, no date of birth, no marital status, no gender, no nationality. Including them looks unprofessional and creates a discrimination risk the employer will simply avoid.
  • Two pages maximum. One if you are early in your career.
  • Achievements with numbers, not duty lists. “Cut month-end close from 9 days to 4” beats “responsible for month-end close”.
  • Convert your qualifications. Write “Bachelor of Engineering (assessed by WES as equivalent to a Canadian bachelor’s degree)”. Do not make them guess.
  • Reverse chronological order, with clear month/year dates.
  • Plain formatting. Most large employers screen with software that mangles columns, tables, headers and graphics.
  • Mirror the posting’s wording for the skills you genuinely have.

Address the elephant directly

Do not hide that you are overseas — they will work it out. Get ahead of it in your cover letter, in concrete terms:

“I am currently in Manila and I am applying under the Atlantic Immigration Program, which lets designated employers hire internationally without an LMIA. My credentials have been assessed by WES, my IELTS result is CLB 8, and I have started registration with the provincial regulator. I can relocate within 90 days of a work permit being issued and I am covering my own relocation costs.”

That paragraph does four things: it names the mechanism that makes hiring you cheap, it proves you have already done the expensive preparation, it gives them a date, and it tells them it costs them nothing. It moves you from “complicated” to “organised”.

Be genuinely ready before you apply

Have your language test done, your ECA in hand, your NOC code identified, and your licensing application started. An employer weighing a nine-month timeline against a candidate who has already removed six months of it is making an easy decision.

Go narrow, not wide

Two hundred generic applications will beat you down and produce nothing. Twenty carefully targeted ones — to designated employers, in a province whose stream you actually qualify for, in an occupation that province lists as in-demand, with a letter naming the program — is a different activity with a different success rate.

What to expect, realistically

  • This takes months, not weeks. Six to eighteen months of consistent effort is normal, and not a sign you are doing it wrong.
  • Silence is the norm. Most applications anywhere get no reply. It is not personal.
  • Interviews will be at awkward hours. Offer to take them at Canadian business hours and say so upfront.
  • Sector matters enormously. Healthcare, trades, trucking, agriculture and construction are genuinely short of people. Generic administrative and management roles are not, and no amount of persistence changes that.
  • Consider whether you need one at all. Saskatchewan’s Occupation In-Demand stream, and the Express Entry draws that Alberta, Nova Scotia, PEI and Newfoundland run, need no job offer. Chasing an offer you do not need is a common and expensive mistake — run the finder before you commit to it.

Recognising scams

Desperation is the product being sold here, and the fraud around Canadian job offers is industrial in scale. Some rules that hold without exception:

Never true, no matter who says it
  • It is illegal in Canada for an employer to charge you for a job or for an LMIA. The employer pays that fee. Anyone asking you for money for an LMIA or a job offer is committing a crime, and the resulting offer is usually fake.
  • Nobody can guarantee a visa, a nomination, or approval. Not a lawyer, not a consultant, not an agency. Those decisions belong to governments.
  • No government charges a fee to a personal bank account, or asks for payment by gift card, cryptocurrency or wire to an individual.
  • A real employer will interview you. An offer that arrives without any assessment of your ability is not real.
  • IRCC does not email you asking for payment to release an application.

Before you pay anyone for advice

Only three categories of person may legally charge for Canadian immigration advice: a licensed immigration consultant (RCIC), a lawyer in good standing with a Canadian provincial law society, or a Quebec notary.

  • Verify an RCIC’s licence on the public CICC register. Do not accept a photo of a certificate.
  • Verify a lawyer with the law society of their province.
  • Ask for a written retainer agreement listing the fee and what it covers.
  • Be suspicious of anyone who discourages you from reading the official government pages yourself.

And to be plain: you do not need to pay anyone. Every program on this site can be applied to directly, by you, for the government fee alone. Paid help can be worth it for complicated cases — a past refusal, a medical issue, a criminal record, a complex family situation — but it is a choice, not a requirement.
