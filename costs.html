Money and time

What it costs, and how long it takes

Government fees are the small part. The larger costs are the tests, assessments and translations nobody mentions up front — and the savings you must prove you have before anyone will approve you. Here is the whole bill.

In one line

A single applicant should budget roughly $4,000–$6,000 CAD in fees and process costs, and must additionally be able to show around $15,300 CAD in savings that they keep. A family of four should budget roughly $7,000–$10,000 in costs and show about $28,400.

Settlement funds — the money you must show but not spend

This one catches people out, because it is not a fee. Most routes require proof that you can support yourself on arrival. You keep the money — you simply have to demonstrate you have it.

1 person$15,263
2 people$19,001
3 people$23,360
4 people$28,362
Each additional personabout $4,112 more

Set at half of Statistics Canada’s Low Income Cut-Off and revised every year, usually in the spring. Confirm the current table before you apply. Provincial streams may set their own figures.

What actually counts

  • Liquid and unencumbered. Cash, current and savings accounts, and readily convertible investments. Property equity does not count. Locked retirement accounts do not count.
  • Yours, and available. Money you cannot withdraw, or that belongs to someone else, does not count.
  • Documented over time. Officers ask for around six months of statements and look at the balance history, not just today’s number. A large deposit shortly before applying invites questions.
  • Not borrowed. Loans and gifts specifically do not qualify, and this is checked.
  • Still there later. You may be asked to prove the funds again when your application is approved, sometimes a year or more after you first showed them. Do not spend them in between.
The main exemption

If you are already working legally in Canada, you are generally exempt from proving settlement funds. This is one of several reasons the routes for people already in Canada are materially easier than the ones from overseas.

Spending it in the right order

If money is tight, the sequence matters. Spend where it unlocks the most.

  1. Language test first (~$330). It gates almost everything and it is the cheapest way to move up any points grid. If you can afford only one thing, this is it.
  2. Credential assessment next (~$250). Slow, so start it early even though the result is not needed until later.
  3. Professional licensing, if regulated. Expensive, slow, and the thing most likely to leave you unable to work in your own field after arriving.
  4. Government fees last. Only due once you are invited, by which point you know the route is real.
  5. Consultants: only if genuinely complicated. A past refusal, a medical issue, a criminal record or an unusual family situation can justify it. A standard application does not.
Sunk costs are real here

Tests expire after two years, credential assessments after five, and provinces close streams without warning. Money spent on a test in 2026 may need spending again in 2028 if your process drags. This is an argument for moving briskly once you start — not for waiting until conditions are perfect.
