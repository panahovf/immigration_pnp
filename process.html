Step by step

The whole process, in order

From knowing nothing to landing as a permanent resident. The order matters more than people expect — several steps take months and depend on each other, and doing them in the wrong sequence is the most common way to lose a year.

The shape of it

You are seeking two separate decisions from two different governments. First a province nominates you. Then the federal government grants permanent residence. The province decides it wants you; only Ottawa can actually admit you. Nearly every confusing thing about this process follows from that split.

Phase one — preparation (2 to 6 months)

All of this happens before you apply to anything, and none of it needs anyone’s permission. Start the slow items on day one and run them in parallel.

  1. Find your NOC code

    Look up the five-digit code matching your real duties, and note its TEER level. Everything downstream keys off this: which streams you can use, what language level you need, what your reference letters must say. How to find it →

  2. Order your credential assessment

    Start this first, because it is the step whose timeline you do not control — it waits on your university sending transcripts. Six weeks to six months. Around $200–$300 CAD. How to get an ECA →

  3. Book and sit a language test

    While the ECA is in progress. Book several weeks ahead, prepare properly, and remember your lowest of four scores is the one that counts. If you are close to a threshold, preparation pays for itself many times over. Which test and what the levels mean →

  4. Collect your reference letters now

    From every employer, on letterhead, with dates, hours, salary and a duties list matching your NOC code. This gets harder every year that passes. What a good letter contains →

  5. Work out which streams you qualify for

    With a NOC code, an assessed credential and a real test score, you can finally match yourself properly against what the provinces are asking for.

    Run the finder →

  6. If your profession is regulated, start licensing

    Separate from immigration and often slower. Begin from overseas. Regulated professions →

Phase two — getting into a pool (1 to 12 months)

  1. Create an Express Entry profile, if you can

    If you meet a federal program’s minimums — broadly CLB 7, an assessed credential and a year of skilled experience — get into the pool even if your score looks hopeless. Provinces fish in this pool, and a nomination adds 600 points. Being in it costs nothing and is the precondition for every “enhanced” stream.

  2. Submit expressions of interest to the provinces you match

    Submit to every stream you qualify for, in every province. They are ranked separately and there is no penalty for breadth. An EOI is not an application — it is a scored profile in a queue. How EOI pools work →

  3. If your streams need a job offer, work at that in parallel

    Do not treat it as a separate phase to start afterwards. Getting a job offer from overseas →

  4. Wait — and use the wait

    This is the stage where people lose momentum. Use it: improve your language score, learn French, gather documents, get police certificates started. Keep your profile accurate, and update it whenever anything changes.

Phase three — nomination (3 to 8 months)

  1. Receive an invitation

    Called an Invitation to Apply, a Letter of Advice to Apply, or a Notification of Interest depending on the province. You typically get 30 to 60 days to submit a complete application. This is why documents are gathered beforehand, not after.

  2. Submit the provincial application

    The full evidence pack: identity documents, the ECA, language results, reference letters, proof of funds, your job offer if the stream needs one, and whatever the province asks to show you intend to settle there. Provincial fees range from nothing to about $1,500 CAD.

  3. Get nominated

    A nomination certificate. If you are in Express Entry, accept it in your profile and 600 points appear on your score. If you are on the direct route, you now have what you need for the federal stage.

Phase four — permanent residence (6 to 24 months)

  1. Apply to the federal government

    A nomination is not permanent residence — it is a provincial endorsement that you must now take to IRCC. Through Express Entry, an invitation follows almost immediately and processing is around six months. On the direct route, expect substantially longer, often eighteen months or more.

  2. Medical exam and police certificates

    The medical must be done by an IRCC-approved panel physician — your own doctor cannot do it. You need a police certificate from every country where you have lived six months or more since turning 18. Some countries take months to issue these, so start early; it is a routine cause of delay.

  3. Biometrics

    Fingerprints and a photograph at a designated collection point after you are asked for them.

  4. Confirmation of permanent residence

    Approval arrives, and with it your Confirmation of Permanent Residence document and, if you need one, a permanent resident visa. There is a deadline to land by — usually tied to your medical’s expiry.

  5. Land in Canada

    You complete the process at a port of entry, where an officer confirms your status. Your PR card is mailed to a Canadian address afterwards. You are now a permanent resident: free to live and work anywhere in Canada, with a path to citizenship after three years of residence.

Mistakes that cost the most

  • Waiting for one step to finish before starting the next. The ECA, the language test and the job search are independent. Run them together.
  • Picking a flattering NOC code. If your reference letters do not describe the duties of the code you claimed, you have a refusal — and possibly a five-year misrepresentation ban.
  • Only checking one province. Different provinces want different people. The finder exists because the right answer is frequently a province you had not considered.
  • Letting documents expire. Language results last two years, ECAs five, police certificates and medicals have their own clocks. Long processes outrun short documents.
  • Assuming a nomination is the finish line. It is roughly halfway.
  • Reading advice that is out of date. Whole streams have closed this year. Always confirm against the official page.
  • Paying someone before checking they are licensed. How to verify →
