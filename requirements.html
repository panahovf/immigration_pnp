Plain-language reference

What these requirements actually mean

Provincial websites state their requirements as if you already know the vocabulary. This page defines each one, tells you what it costs, how long it takes, and how to go and get it.

If you only read one thing

Three items gate nearly every stream in Canada: a language test, an educational credential assessment, and knowing your NOC code. None of them requires a job offer, a lawyer, or permission from anyone. Start all three now, in parallel — they take weeks to months and everything else waits on them.

NOC code and TEER level

Seen as: “your occupation must be NOC TEER 0, 1, 2 or 3”

Canada does not care what your job title says. It cares which of roughly 500 official occupation codes your actual duties match. That five-digit code is your NOC code (National Occupational Classification), and its first digit is the TEER level — how much training the job needs.

TEERWhat it meansExamples
0ManagementRestaurant manager, construction manager, financial manager
1Needs a university degreeDoctor, software engineer, accountant, architect
2College diploma, or 2+ years apprenticeship, or supervisoryRegistered nurse, electrician, paralegal, computer technician
3College under 2 years, or 6 months on-the-job trainingLong-haul truck driver, dental assistant, cook, bookkeeper
4High school, or several weeks of trainingHome care worker, food service supervisor, retail salesperson
5Short work demonstration, no formal educationKitchen helper, harvesting labourer, cleaner

How to find yours

  1. Search the official NOC tool for your job title.
  2. Open the codes that look close and read the “main duties” list. This is the part that counts.
  3. Pick the code where you genuinely performed most of the listed duties — not the one with the nicest title or the best TEER level.

Get this right the first time

Choosing a flattering code rather than an accurate one is one of the most common reasons applications are refused, and a refusal for misrepresentation can bar you from applying for five years. Your reference letters must describe duties that match the code you claim. Pick the code that matches what you really did.

Language tests, CLB and NCLC

Seen as: “CLB 7 in all four abilities” or “NCLC 5”

CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) is a 1–12 scale Canada uses to compare different English tests on one ruler. NCLC is exactly the same thing for French. “All four abilities” means reading, writing, listening and speaking are scored separately, and your lowest score is the one that counts.

Self-assessment is worth nothing here. You need an approved test, taken at an approved centre, and the result is valid for two years.

CLBIELTS General Training (each band)Roughly meansOpens
4L 4.5 / R 3.5 / W 4.0 / S 4.0Basic, can handle routine tasksLower-skilled and some direct provincial streams
5L 5.0 / R 4.0 / W 5.0 / S 5.0Modest working abilityMany direct provincial streams
76.0 in every bandComfortable at workThe federal Express Entry pool
9L 8.0 / R 7.0 / W 7.0 / S 7.0StrongLarge score bonuses; competitive federal draws

Which test to take

  • IELTS General Training — the most widely available. Make sure it is General Training, not Academic; Academic is not accepted for immigration.
  • CELPIP General — fully computer-based, Canadian English, results in a few days. Only available in some countries.
  • PTE Core — computer-based, fast results, often cheaper.
  • TEF Canada or TCF Canada — for French. These are the only two accepted.

Expect roughly $300–$350 CAD, and book several weeks ahead.

The French point, made properly

If you can reach about NCLC 5 in French, entire routes open that are effectively shut to English-only candidates: the Francophone Community Immigration Pilot, New Brunswick’s francophone stream, the Northwest Territories francophone stream, and federal French-language draws that have consistently had among the lowest cut-offs of any draw.

Canada has a standing policy objective to increase French-speaking immigration outside Quebec, and it is well behind its targets. That gap is why these routes stay open. For someone starting from scratch with no job offer and no Canadian ties, learning French is often the single highest-return investment available — more than another degree, and far more than any consultant.

Educational Credential Assessment (ECA)

Seen as: “an ECA from a designated organisation, less than five years old”

A report from an approved agency saying what your foreign qualification is worth in Canadian terms. Your Indian three-year B.Com., for instance, might come back as “equivalent to a three-year Canadian bachelor’s degree” — or as a diploma. You do not get to assume; the report decides.

How to get one

  1. Pick a designated organisation. WES is the most commonly used and usually the fastest; IQAS, ICES, CES and ICAS are also designated. For doctors and pharmacists, specific bodies are required instead.
  2. Create an account and pay. Budget $200–$300 CAD plus courier costs.
  3. Ask your university to send transcripts directly to the agency. This is the step that causes almost all the delay, because it depends on an institution that has no stake in your timeline. Start chasing it immediately.

Allow six weeks to six months, dominated by how fast your university responds. The result is valid for five years.

Do this first, not last

An ECA blocks almost everything downstream and its timeline is out of your hands. Order it on day one, then get on with your language test while you wait.

Work experience — how it is counted and proved

Seen as: “two years of full-time experience in the same NOC within the past five years”

Rules vary, but the pattern is consistent:

  • Paid only. Volunteering, unpaid internships and self-directed study do not count.
  • Full-time means 30 hours a week. Part-time counts proportionally — 15 hours a week for two years is one year of experience.
  • Continuous in one occupation. Experience scattered across unrelated roles does not aggregate.
  • Within a window, usually the last five or ten years.

What proof looks like

The document that matters is a reference letter from each employer, on company letterhead, signed, with contact details, stating:

  • Your job title, and the exact dates you were employed
  • Hours per week and your salary
  • A list of your actual duties — and these must line up with the NOC code you are claiming

Back it with contracts, payslips and tax records where you can.

Ask before you leave

Getting a detailed reference letter out of a former employer years later is painful, and sometimes impossible if the manager has moved on or the company has closed. If you are even considering emigrating, ask for the letter now, in the form above, from every employer you have. It costs you one email and saves an application later.

“A connection to the province”

Seen as: “you must demonstrate a close connection to Manitoba”

Some provinces — Manitoba most strictly — will not consider you at all unless you can document a real tie. They do this because nominees who have no roots in a province tend to leave for Toronto or Vancouver soon after landing.

What typically counts:

  • A close relative settled there — parent, sibling, aunt, uncle, niece, nephew or first cousin who is a citizen or permanent resident and has lived in the province for at least a year. This is the strongest form.
  • Your own previous authorised work there, often six months or more.
  • Your own previous full-time study there, often a year or more.
  • Being recruited directly by the province at an overseas recruitment mission or job fair.
  • A job offer from an employer in the province, which is its own form of connection.

What usually does not: wanting to live there, having visited, having a friend there (some provinces accept a supporting friend but weight it very lightly), or a relative who is themselves a temporary resident.

If you have no connection anywhere, do not despair — target the streams that do not ask for one. The finder will tell you which those are.

Letters of intent, settlement plans and intent to reside

Seen as: “a letter of intent”, “a settlement plan”, “demonstrated intention to reside”

Three different things that sound alike:

Intent to reside

A declaration that you genuinely plan to live in the nominating province. As of 2026 the provinces themselves — not federal officers — assess this. It is not a formality: they look for evidence such as your job search history there, family ties, property, or professional registration. Nominating provinces can and do refuse people whose stated intention is not credible. You are legally free to move after landing, but stating an intention you do not hold is misrepresentation.

Settlement plan

Required by the Atlantic Immigration Program. You meet a funded settlement organisation, usually online, and they help you produce a needs assessment and plan covering housing, schooling, credential recognition and community connections. It is free, and it is genuinely useful rather than bureaucratic.

Letter of intent / community recommendation

In rural and community-based programs, the community itself must recommend or endorse you. You typically approach the community’s economic development organisation once you have a job offer from a local designated employer. Each community publishes its own contact and process, and they are usually small offices that answer emails from real people.

Expression of Interest (EOI) pools

Seen as: “submit an EOI”, “you may receive a Letter of Advice to Apply”

An EOI is not an application. It is a short profile that gets you scored and dropped into a ranked pool. Periodically the province runs a draw, takes the top scorers, and invites them to actually apply.

Three consequences people miss:

  • Meeting the minimum is not the goal. Minimums get you into the pool. Cut-offs are set by whoever else is in the pool that month, and run far higher.
  • Profiles expire — typically after a year — and some provinces do not carry them over between intake windows. Yukon, for instance, wipes the pool between its two annual intakes.
  • Every point is worth chasing, because you are competing on the margin. Another half-band on your language test is usually the cheapest way to move up.

The invitation itself has various names — Invitation to Apply, Letter of Advice to Apply, Notification of Interest, Letter of Interest. They all mean the same thing: you may now submit a real application, usually within a tight deadline of 30 to 60 days. Have your documents ready before you are invited, not after.

Provincial points grids

Each province scores its pool differently, but the ingredients barely vary: age, education, language, work experience, and connection to the province. Some add wage, region or occupation.

Two practical observations:

  • Language is almost always the cheapest points. Retaking a test costs a few hundred dollars and weeks. Another degree costs years.
  • Age points decline from your early thirties. If you are approaching a threshold, the value of moving quickly is real and quantifiable.

Do not confuse a provincial grid with the federal CRS score. They are separate systems with separate scales that happen to look similar.

How Express Entry and PNP fit together

Express Entry is the federal pool. To enter it you must qualify for one of three federal programs — in practice, for most people that means about CLB 7, a post-secondary credential with an ECA, and a year of skilled experience. Everyone in the pool has a CRS score out of roughly 1,200, and the federal government periodically invites the top scorers.

A provincial nomination is worth 600 CRS points. That is why it matters so much: it lifts essentially anyone above any plausible cut-off.

So there are two shapes to a PNP application:

  • Enhanced — you are already in the Express Entry pool, the province nominates you there, you get 600 points, and you are invited within weeks. Fast.
  • Direct (base) — you apply straight to the province, outside Express Entry entirely. Slower afterwards, but open to people who cannot meet federal minimums at all.
A job offer no longer raises your federal score

Until March 2025, a valid job offer added 50 or 200 CRS points. Those points were removed over fraud concerns and, as of September 2026, have not returned — a proposal to reinstate them for high-wage roles is still only a proposal. A job offer remains the key to most provincial streams, and it is still how you get a work permit. But anyone offering to sell you an LMIA-backed job offer to raise your CRS score is selling something that does not work.

Regulated professions and licensing

Roughly a fifth of Canadian jobs are regulated by a provincial body, and you cannot legally practise — or often even be hired — until it licenses you. This catches doctors, nurses, pharmacists, engineers, teachers, lawyers, accountants, electricians and many trades.

Two things to understand:

  • Licensing is provincial, not national. Registration in Ontario is not registration in Alberta, though there are transfer arrangements.
  • It is separate from immigration. Nothing in your permanent residence application licenses you to work. People do arrive as permanent residents and then discover they cannot practise for two years.

Start the licensing process from overseas, before you move. Most regulators let you begin credential review remotely, and several provinces now fund bridging programs for internationally trained professionals. For healthcare especially, provinces are actively easing these routes because the shortages are severe.

How entrepreneur streams really work

These are frequently misrepresented as a way to buy permanent residence. They are not.

The usual shape: you submit a business plan and prove your net worth; if selected you sign a performance agreement committing to invest a set amount, create jobs and actively manage the business; you then move to Canada on a temporary work permit; and only after you have actually run the business for a year or two and met the agreement are you nominated.

So you are committing capital and years of your life before you have any status security. Thresholds commonly start around $150,000–$600,000 CAD depending on province and location, with rural streams cheaper than urban ones. For most people, the worker routes are faster, cheaper and far less risky.

Routes that get you into Canada first

Many provincial streams are only open to people already in the province. If your results are full of those, the strategic question becomes how to get here temporarily and convert later. The realistic options:

  • A work permit, which almost always needs an employer — either through an LMIA, or an LMIA-exempt route such as an intra-company transfer or a free-trade-agreement permit. The job offer page covers this.
  • Working Holiday (International Experience Canada), if you are under 31 or 36 and hold a passport from a participating country. An open work permit with no employer needed — comfortably the easiest way in if you are eligible, and badly underused. Check whether your country has a youth mobility agreement with Canada.
  • Study, covered below.
  • An intra-company transfer, if your current employer has a Canadian entity. If this applies to you it is often the single easiest route available.

The study-then-immigrate route, honestly

Studying in Canada and then applying provincially is a real and well-trodden path. It is also expensive and has become considerably less certain.

The case for: you finish with a Canadian credential, Canadian work experience through a post-graduation work permit, and access to graduate streams — a combination that makes you competitive almost everywhere.

The case against: international tuition typically runs $20,000–$40,000 CAD a year before living costs; Canada capped study permits and tightened post-graduation work permit eligibility from 2024 onwards; and several provinces have since closed their graduate streams entirely — British Columbia scrapped its International Graduate stream in 2026, and Ontario closed its Masters and PhD Graduate streams. Programs that existed when a student enrolled have disappeared before they graduated.

If you are considering this

Choose a program only if the qualification is worth having on its own merits, and confirm that the specific credential leads to a post-graduation work permit before you pay anything. Do not treat a study permit as a purchased immigration outcome — on current evidence, it is not one. Agents who pitch it that way are selling a commission, not a plan.
